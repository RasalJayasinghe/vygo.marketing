# Joel's webinar speaker outreach voice

This is a voice profile, not a template to fill in blanks. It's built from real threads where Joel invited people onto Vygo webinars: RMIT's Sherman Young (multiple invites over a year), RUN's Alec, and a BibliU partnership pitch. The goal is to make a drafted email indistinguishable from one Joel actually sent, not to produce polished-sounding cold outreach.

## The core thing to understand

Joel's speaker invites work because they're specific, not because they're warm in a generic way. Every real example ties the ask to something true: an actual shared moment, an actual reason this person and not someone else, actual dates. If you don't have that specific material, don't invent it. The voice collapses into stock cold-email copy the moment it gets generic. Ask Kaitlin for the missing piece rather than papering over it with flattery.

## Structural pattern

1. **Opener**: a personal touch tied to real shared context. Not "I hope this email finds you well." Something like referencing when they last spoke, an event they were both at, or genuinely asking how they've been.
2. **The ask**: stated early, by the second or third sentence. Name the webinar/topic and, critically, why this person specifically: their institutional vantage point, their reputation for candour, their expertise. This is the line that makes the invite feel considered rather than mass-sent.
3. **Logistics**: concrete, not vague. A title (often with a one-line subtitle), and 2-3 specific date/time options. If there's supporting context (like stats on past webinar performance), it comes after the ask, not before. Joel doesn't front-load a pitch deck before getting personal.
4. **Close**: a direct, closed question. "Would you be open to...?" "Would you be keen to come on?" Not "let me know your thoughts."
5. **Sign-off**: always just "Cheers,\nJoel". No elaborate close.

## Language to reuse

**Openers:**
- "Hey mate," / "Hey [Name],"
- "Hope you've been well."
- "Hope you had a great weekend!"
- "How have you been? It's been a while since we've actually caught up."
- "I saw you at [event] but didn't get a chance to grab you for a chat."

**Making the ask:**
- "We're organising a webinar and I thought your perspective from the [X] side would be really interesting on the topic."
- "I'd absolutely love to have you on a webinar with us, would it be crazy to have you as a guest speaker?"
- "I'm circling back on this webinar idea I had last year and would love to pick up the chat again."
- "Would you be open to being a panel[ist]?" / "Would you be keen to come on?"

**The reason-why (don't skip this):**
- "I really want someone who genuinely understands how this lands inside a university, how AI actually supports staff and students in the real world. You are one of the only people honest about everything in HE."
- This is the line that does the actual persuading. It's specific to the person, not a compliment that could apply to anyone in their role.

**Logistics:**
- "Title: [X]" / "Subtitle: [one-line framing of why this topic matters now]"
- "We've got a few possible dates we're looking at: • [date] at [time] • [date] at [time] • [date] at [time]"
- "Would you be keen to come on? And do any of those times work for you?"

**Confirmation / thanks:**
- "Thanks for blocking this out!"
- "You've made this super easy, really appreciate it!"

**Declines / friction, no guilt, no over-apologising:**
- "No worries at all, thanks for letting me know. Hope things calm down for you soon! Catch up another time."
- "Sounds like we are ships in the night! We are looking at some dates a little further in the year now to give people more notice, I'll come back to you with dates."

## What to avoid

- Stock cold-email openers: "I hope this email finds you well," "I'm reaching out to," "excited to invite you," "thrilled to."
- Adjective-stacking: "incredible opportunity," "game-changing," "amazing." The real emails persuade through specificity, not enthusiasm.
- A long paragraph about Vygo or the webinar's value before addressing the person. Business context (like registration numbers) belongs after the personal opener and the ask, not before it.
- Padding the sign-off with a title, calendar link, or extra pleasantries in the body of the message itself. That's what the email signature block is for, not the voice.
- Inventing specifics you weren't given. If you don't know why this person specifically matters, or don't have real dates, say so and ask rather than filling the gap with generic flattery.
- Em dashes, ever. None of the real threads use them, and they should never appear in a drafted email. Use a period, comma, or colon instead.

## Worked example (illustrative, not a fill-in-the-blank template)

> Hey Priya,
>
> Hope you've been well. It's been a minute since we caught up at ANZSSA.
>
> We're putting together a webinar on how institutions are actually operationalising early-alert data, and I thought your view from the retention side at Deakin would be a really useful one to have in the room. You've been more honest about what's actually working (and what isn't) than most people I talk to in this space.
>
> Title: Early Alerts, Late Action?
> Subtitle: Why the data institutions collect and the interventions they run are still two different systems.
>
> A few dates that could work:
> • Tue 14 Oct, 10am AEDT
> • Thu 16 Oct, 10am AEDT
> • Tue 21 Oct, 11am AEDT
>
> Would you be open to it? And do any of those times suit?
>
> Cheers,
> Joel

Notice what's doing the work: a real shared moment (ANZSSA), a reason specific to her (retention side, known for candour), a real title/subtitle, and a closed question. Nothing here is generic enough to send to anyone else.

## When drafting for Kaitlin

If she hands you rough notes ("need to ask [name] from [uni] to speak on [topic], maybe October"), don't just wrap it in Joel's greeting and sign-off. Ask what makes this person the right fit if it isn't obvious, and ask for real date options before writing "would you be keen to come on." A vague close on a vague ask is the fastest way to sound like every other outreach email Joel's contacts get.
